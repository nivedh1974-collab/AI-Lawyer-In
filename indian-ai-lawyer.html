Indian AI Lawyer

A chatbot that answers Indian legal questions from the actual Acts and cites the section. When the Acts don't cover the question, it says so.

Try the live app Solo project. Python, LangChain, ChromaDB.

Indian AI Lawyer answers questions from 14 Indian Acts, including the BNS, BNSS and BSA that replaced the IPC, CrPC and Evidence Act in 2024. You ask in plain English. It finds the relevant passages, answers from them, and names the Act and section. If the passages don't support an answer, it declines instead of guessing.

Why I built it

Three criminal laws changed in 2024. The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS) replaced the IPC, the BNSS replaced the CrPC, and the BSA replaced the Evidence Act. Many tools still answer from the old sections. I hit the same problem in my own build: before I tightened the prompt, the app cited IPC sections from the model's memory.

That is a problem for two kinds of people.

  • Someone with a legal question and no lawyer. A fine, a notice, an arrest of a relative. They can't tell whether an AI answer comes from the law or from guesswork.
  • Law students and researchers. They need to find a section fast and verify that the citation is real.

The problem isn't access to AI. It's trust.

A legal answer with no citation is an opinion. People need an AI that shows its work: which Act and which section.

How it works

I loaded 14 Acts as PDFs, split them into chunks of 800 characters with a 100-character overlap, and stored each chunk in ChromaDB with the name of its Act.

  1. You ask in plain English

    For example, "What is the punishment for theft?"

  2. The question becomes a vector

    A sentence-transformers model (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) turns it into numbers that capture its meaning.

  3. The closest passages are pulled

    ChromaDB returns the stored chunks nearest in meaning to the question.

  4. The model answers from those passages only

    GPT-OSS 120B on Groq reads them and writes the answer with the Act name and section number.

  5. If the passages don't support an answer, it refuses

    The reply is a fixed line telling the user to consult a licensed advocate.

Decisions and trade-offs

Refuse instead of guess

A wrong legal answer costs the user more than no answer. The cost shows up in the test: 3 of 23 answerable questions were declined, 13%. I accepted that trade for the first version.

New criminal laws first

BNS, BNSS and BSA were indexed first because they replaced the IPC, CrPC and Evidence Act. The other 11 Acts, from the Constitution to the Kerala Motor Vehicles Act, cover everyday situations: contracts, property sales, marriage, consumer complaints, RTI requests, bounced cheques, corruption, domestic violence, cybercrime and Kerala traffic rules.

Ground the answer in retrieved text

A plain chatbot answers from memory, which is how an old IPC section ends up in a BNS answer. Retrieval puts the actual text in front of the model before it writes anything.

Model choice

I started on Llama 3.3 70B. When it stopped being available on my Groq key, I moved to GPT-OSS 120B, which was available on the same key.

What broke, and what I changed

The app returned a 404

What happened

The model I started with, Llama 3.3 70B, stopped being available on my Groq key.

What I did

Switched to GPT-OSS 120B, which is available on my key and answered the BNS test question correctly.

Every answer was a refusal

What happened

I had never uploaded the vector database to the hosting space. Retrieval found nothing, so the app declined every question.

What I did

Rebuilt the database from the current PDFs and uploaded it. It also confirmed the refusal rule works: with no sources, the app refuses instead of guessing.

It cited the IPC

What happened

Answers pointed to IPC sections even though only the new Acts are indexed. The model was filling in from memory.

What I did

Rewrote the instructions to require BNS, BNSS and BSA and to forbid IPC citations from memory. The next answer cited BNS 103 and 104.

Correct, but thin

What happened

Answers were right but read like a pair of quotes with no explanation.

What I did

Changed the answer format to a short plain answer first, then the section, then related provisions.

I tested it on 27 questions and scored every answer by hand.

The questions cover all 14 indexed Acts: 21 in scope, 3 out of scope, and 3 edge cases (an outdated IPC reference, a question that spans two Acts, and a prompt injection attempt). I checked each answer against the source text.

20 / 23answers matched the source documents (87%)
20 / 23answers had the right Act name and section number (87%)
4 / 4out-of-scope and adversarial questions refused, with no invented citations
3 / 23good questions wrongly declined (13%)

The three misses were the FIR filing procedure, the Kerala helmet rule, and the IPC 302 to BNS mapping. The system declined all three instead of finding the section. The full log is further down.

The full test log

Each answer was scored on three things: whether the content was correct, whether the Act and section were right, and whether the system answered or refused as it should. Misses are shaded.

The eval report attributes the three misses to a retrieval-precision issue on specific procedural and cross-referencing questions, not a reasoning failure: on the FIR, helmet and IPC mapping questions, the right section never reached the model. That is the eval's own read of the pattern, worth treating as a hypothesis rather than a confirmed root cause until retrieval is inspected directly.

Limits and what's next

  • Small test set. 27 questions, scored by me, not blind. It shows the direction, not a guarantee.
  • Limited coverage. It knows 14 Acts. Anything else gets a refusal.
  • Search can miss exact sections. Matching by meaning alone doesn't always find an exact section number when the question never says it.
  • Not legal advice. It is a way to find and read the law, and it tells the user to consult an advocate when unsure.

Next: add keyword search alongside meaning search to fix the three misses, grow the test set past 27, and rerun the same questions to compare against 20 of 23.
